# Evaluation of skoro's core

*Written 2026-11-10 against `main` at `0ce92b2`, from the documentation (`site/build/llms-full.txt`), the source of the seven crates, and two experiments run in a scratch crate outside the repository. Timings are from a release build on the host target in double precision, on one machine; read them as proportions, not absolutes.*

## Verdict

The core holds up. Its main positions (a model is the joint and conditioning belongs to the query; each type declares its reference measure; an algorithm refuses by structure; draws come from keys) are consistent with each other and with the two examples, and I found nothing in them that the planned work (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, then the 0.1.0 release) will have to undo. The rule that the normal requires a global chart is a good compromise and I would keep it. It has three consequences that deserve attention before the release, two of which a user can hit with no warning. Apart from the normal there are four further findings: a defect in retirement that matters for any long-running model, a measured cost of running a single estimator, and two limits that the next distributions and samplers will meet. The last section says what I would do first.

## The normal and its global chart

skoro's normal on a space is the law of `μ ⊕ ε`: the mean moved through the space's retraction by noise `ε` that is Gaussian in tangent coordinates. Its log-density is the Gaussian log-density of `x ⊖ μ` minus the chart's log-volume, so that the density and the sampler describe exactly the same law. That is only possible when the chart at `μ` maps all of `ℝ^d` one-to-one onto the space, less a set of measure zero, which is what "global" means, and `Normal::score` refuses any other space. For rotations this ruled out the exponential chart, whose noise wraps around, and led to unit quaternions with a stereographic chart, in which a tangent of length `r` is a turn by `4·arctan(r/4)`. The trade is sound. Importance weights are exact, the formulas are rational with no trigonometry, and the chart differs from rotation vectors only by a term of `θ³/48`, so a covariance taken from another library means the same thing at ordinary noise levels.

The first consequence is that the type being modelled is the unit quaternions, not the rotations. `q` and `-q` are one rotation but two points, the normal tells them apart, and the caller must give an observed quaternion the sign of its prediction (`host::align`). A quaternion with the wrong sign is not a slightly worse fit. It sits beside the one point the chart cannot reach, where the residual grows without bound; nothing refuses it; and no optimizer step can cross to the other sign, because that point is at infinity in the chart. Both examples draw their measurements from the model, so their signs are right by construction, and this path has never been exercised with outside data. The same double cover gives a posterior two mirrored modes whenever the attitude enters only through the rotation it represents. Convergence diagnostics for Hamiltonian Monte Carlo will read chains on opposite signs as disagreeing unless they are computed on functions that ignore the sign.

The second consequence is that a broad normal on a rotation is not a weak prior. The documentation says that the mean stops being a mode once the standard deviation passes about 1.6 rad. It does not say where the mass then goes: to the far side of the quaternion sphere, which as a rotation is the mean again. Drawing from `Normal::on(Rotation, identity, Scale::sd(σ))`, the median angle between the drawn rotation and the mean was 1.47 rad at σ = 1, 2.48 rad at σ = 3, 1.01 rad at σ = 10, 0.10 rad at σ = 100 and 0.010 rad at σ = 1000. So the common habit of writing a very large standard deviation to mean "unknown" produces a tight prior, and during optimization such a term pushes the estimate away from its mean. A uniform distribution on these types is the right way to say "unknown", and a constant scale above about one radian on a curved type deserves a refusal, or at least a prominent note.

The third consequence is structural: a type has one retraction, and it is both how an optimizer steps and what every normal on the type means. A pose with noise in its own frame is the type `Pose`; one with position noise in a fixed frame is a product of a rotation and a vector; noise given in the group's exponential coordinates has no type. A type whose natural chart is not global can hold a measurement only as a likelihood factor (`factor_at`), which has no sampler, so that part of the model can no longer be generated. This is acceptable today, since the three spatial types cover the examples. If it starts to bind, the cheaper way out is to let `Normal` take its chart as an argument that defaults to the type's, not to relax the global-chart rule. One note for readers of the docs: the live page on the normal still gives the Cayley chart as its example for the rotations, and the figures in the normal's archived mathematics are the Cayley chart's. The ones that apply to `skoro-spatial` are in the rotations entry.

## Findings outside the normal

**Retirement does not free constants.** `Retirement::replace` removes the retired quantities and every node computed from them (`remove` in `crates/skoro-core/src/retire.rs`). A constant, or a value folded from constants, is computed from no quantity, so it is never removed: scales, anchors, and each replacement's matrix and right-hand side stay in the model after the factor that used them is gone. Running the loop of the `sliding_window` example, the model held 854 nodes after tick 9 and 4,121 after tick 79, while its quantities and terms stayed between 33 and 50. Compiling walks back from a query's targets and does not visit these nodes, so the cost is memory, not time, but it is unbounded in a library built for models that run indefinitely. The fix is small (drop fixed nodes that a retirement leaves with no consumer), with one decision to make: whether a handle the user still holds keeps a constant alive.

**A single row is slow.** In the same loop, with a window of five poses, one linearization took 10.5 ms for one row and 56.5 ms for a hundred rows, and one elimination with its step took 0.19 ms for one row. A first linearization on a new snapshot cost the same as a second, so the time is not compilation. A tick's Levenberg–Marquardt solve took 95 ms for one row and 657 ms for a hundred. The cost is therefore mostly a fixed overhead of running each program's many small steps on the tensor backend, which a batch of rows shares and a single row pays in full. This suits many datasets or hypotheses at once. It does not yet suit one estimator running in real time, which the docs name as an audience. It is not a blocker for 0.1.0, but the README should not claim real-time use until it is addressed; fused kernels, already in `PLAN.md`, are the lever.

**Estimation reads only Gaussian-shaped terms.** `Linearizer` refuses a model with any term that binds no residual (`NoResidual`), and the roles it reads are the normal's. The log-volume role has already been redefined as "the varying part of a remainder" to carry the improper factor's linear part, which shows the strain. The second distribution will meet this at once. One term that is not a normal, such as the half-Cauchy prior on the scale that the eight-schools example usually carries, makes the whole model unlinearizable, and with it the plan's sequence of a mode, then a Laplace approximation, then Hamiltonian Monte Carlo. Robust losses, which real pose-graph data needs, have no route either. A term without a residual needs a fallback, such as contributing its gradient and Hessian products through the core's second-order passes.

**Draws are made on the host.** Every sampler runs on the host in double precision, with one chain of keyed hashes per row and quantity, and enters a generation program as a step with no derivative. This is deliberate and gives reproducible draws at any precision. Its costs come later: particle methods on a GPU pay a round trip per drawn quantity, and no gradient passes through a draw, so variational methods that differentiate through samples would need the noise as an explicit input.

## Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

*Added after the rest, to answer whether Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo needs much more of the core. This section is analysis of the documented passes and declarations. Nothing in it was run.*

It needs little, and it needs no third-order derivatives, provided two choices are made as described below. The name covers two different things, which ask different things of the core: sampling quantities that live on curved spaces, and giving the sampler a metric that changes from point to point. The memory of metrics and third derivatives belongs to the second.

**Curved quantities.** `PLAN.md` worries that leapfrog through a retraction on the rotations is neither reversible nor volume-preserving. That is true when the chart moves with the state, and it stops being true when the chart is fixed. A global chart at one anchor `a` covers the whole type but a set of measure zero, so the map `t ↦ a ⊕ t` turns the problem into ordinary Hamiltonian Monte Carlo on `ℝ^d`, with the chart's log-volume added to the log-density as the Jacobian. The first Hamiltonian Monte Carlo entry then applies unchanged. This is what the global-chart rule buys beyond the normal. It asks nothing new of the core: the gradient in the fixed chart is the gradient the core already gives in the point's own chart, carried through the Jacobian of the change of chart, which is one tangent pass over the type's own retraction and inverse. The stereographic chart suits it well. It is conformal, and lengths in it shrink by `cos²(θ/4)` at a turn of `θ` from the anchor, so by a factor of two at most over every rotation within a half-turn. The anchor is the mode, and the Laplace covariance, which is already given in that chart, is the mass matrix. Its limits are a posterior that truly spreads over all rotations, and the mirrored mode on the far side of the quaternion sphere, which the chain will rarely visit.

The alternative is an integrator that follows geodesics, which is what the literature on manifolds usually means. The core is closer to it than it looks. Every type's reference measure is, by the note on the measure a retraction normalizes, the Riemannian volume of the metric in which the retraction's bases are orthonormal. So a metric is already implied, the gradient a pass returns is the Riemannian gradient in an orthonormal frame, and a density against the reference measure needs no correction. What is missing is the geodesic itself: a type would declare a step along it that also carries the momentum to the new point's frame, as a type declares its volume today, and it needs no derivative. On the rotations the mass matrix of such an integrator must be a multiple of the identity, since other left-invariant metrics have geodesics with no simple form. I would take the fixed chart first and keep this in reserve.

**A metric that varies.** This is Riemannian manifold Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in the strict sense, and its purpose is a posterior whose scale changes by orders of magnitude, such as the funnel of eight schools. The integrator needs a metric `G` at each point, its log-determinant, solves against it, and the derivative of `G`. That derivative costs one order more than whatever `G` is built from. A metric built from the Hessian of the log-density therefore needs third derivatives, which would mean a fourth variant of `Kernel` and a third-order rule for every primitive. That is the large change, and I would not make it. The Fisher linearization already assembles a positive semidefinite `G = JᵀJ` from first derivatives of the residuals and from the information roots, and it adapts to a free scale, which is what a funnel needs. For a fixed direction `u`, the derivative of `½|J u|²` is one tangent pass followed by one Hessian pass seeded with `J u`, both of which exist. The log-determinant and the solves are answers `skoro-elimination` already gives.

What remains is mostly outside the core: an implicit integrator whose fixed-point iterations stop row by row, and the derivative of the log-determinant, which takes one second-order pass per coordinate. The one change to the core worth considering is a Hessian pass that takes a seed for each velocity column, so that this trace costs one pass. Its cost also multiplies the single-row overhead measured above. A cheaper cure for a funnel deserves weighing in the same entry: sampling in the whitened residual that every normal already binds, which is the non-centred parameterization read off the model, and which needs the cuts of `PLAN.md` and no metric.

## What I would do first

None of this has been started; each item would be its own workflow entry. First, free constants at retirement, before anything long-running is shown. Second, add a test or example that observes quaternions with arbitrary signs, including a loop closure given with the opposite sign to its prediction, and guard or document broad scales on curved types; a uniform distribution on the rotations fits naturally in the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo entry. Third, settle the fallback for terms without a residual as part of that same entry, since its eight-schools example needs a mode and a Laplace approximation to start from. Fourth, measure the single-row cost again before the README is written. The chart belonging to the type, and sampling on the host, can stay as they are. For Riemannian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, curved quantities in a fixed chart follow the first sampler directly and need nothing new from the core, and a varying metric comes later, built on the Fisher linearization and never on third derivatives.
